# Wild West Showdown 3D

Build a complete, polished and genuinely fun 3D Wild West action game.

The game should evoke the feeling of fast arcade cowboy duels and blocky multiplayer games, while using an original visual identity and no copyrighted Roblox assets, branding, UI or characters.

The setting is a stylized western desert town populated by blocky characters.

The final result must feel like a small finished game rather than a technical demo.

It must run in a modern web browser and be fully playable on desktop and touch devices, with iPad/tablet as a first-class target.

Use Three.js for rendering. You may use additional lightweight libraries when they genuinely improve the implementation.

# DEVELOPMENT RULE

Build vertically.

Do not spend most of the project creating isolated systems that have never been played together.

Get a minimal complete gameplay loop working early:

walk → find opponent → duel → shoot → win/lose → reward → continue.

# GAME CONCEPT

The player is a blocky cowboy arriving in a lively frontier town.

The town contains rival gunslingers.

The player explores the environment, approaches opponents and challenges them to quick Wild West duels.

Winning duels earns money and reputation.

As the player progresses, opponents become faster and more dangerous.

The ultimate objective is to defeat the town's most feared gunslinger in a final showdown.

The overall experience should be accessible, humorous and arcade-like rather than realistic or violent.

Avoid gore.

---

# WORLD

Create a coherent explorable Wild West desert town.

It should feel intentionally designed rather than like randomly placed primitives.

Include at minimum:

- main street,
- saloon,
- sheriff's office,
- bank,
- general store,
- stable,
- water tower,
- small houses or cabins,
- wooden fences,
- barrels and crates,
- hitching posts,
- desert rocks,
- cactus,
- distant mesas or mountains.

Not every building needs an interior.

Use environmental composition to make the town easy to understand and navigate.

The town should have recognizable landmarks.

The desert should extend visually beyond the playable area so the world does not appear to end abruptly.

Invisible or natural boundaries should prevent the player from wandering indefinitely into empty terrain.

---

# VISUAL STYLE

Use an original stylized blocky aesthetic.

Characters should have deliberately simple, chunky proportions:

- rectangular torso,
- blocky arms,
- blocky legs,
- simplified head,
- cowboy hat,
- simple facial features.

Characters should be readable and expressive without requiring external character assets.

Use warm western colors:

- dusty earth,
- sand,
- weathered wood,
- faded paint,
- strong sunlight,
- blue sky.

Add visual atmosphere such as:

- drifting dust,
- tumbleweeds,
- chimney smoke,
- swinging signs,
- birds in the distance,
- subtle wind movement.

Keep performance appropriate for tablet hardware.

---

# PLAYER

Create a third-person controllable cowboy.

The player must be able to:

- walk,
- run,
- turn,
- aim,
- shoot,
- interact with duel opponents.

Movement should feel responsive and arcade-like.

The player should not be able to leave the intended playable area.

---

# DESKTOP CONTROLS

At minimum:

- WASD or arrow keys: move
- Mouse drag or mouse movement: camera
- Left mouse button: shoot when appropriate
- E or equivalent: interact/challenge
- Escape: pause

You may improve the exact mapping when useful.

---

# MOBILE / TABLET CONTROLS

The entire game must be playable without a keyboard or mouse.

Tablet landscape is the primary touch target.

Provide:

- virtual movement joystick on the left,
- camera control by dragging on a clear area of the right side,
- large context-sensitive action/shoot button,
- interaction control when needed.

Multi-touch must work correctly.

The player must be able to:

- move while rotating the camera,
- move while using an action control,
- aim and shoot comfortably.

Touch interaction must not accidentally scroll, zoom or select the page.

Controls must be large enough to use comfortably on an iPad.

Do not simply make desktop buttons visible on mobile and call that touch support.

Actually design the interaction for touch.

---

# THIRD-PERSON CAMERA

Use a polished third-person camera.

It should:

- follow smoothly,
- rotate around the player,
- allow limited vertical movement,
- prevent upside-down rotation,
- maintain a useful distance,
- keep the character readable,
- avoid excessive clipping through buildings.

During normal exploration the player controls the camera.

During a formal duel, the game may temporarily take more control of framing in order to produce a cinematic confrontation.

Transitions between exploration and duel cameras should be smooth.

---

# OPPONENTS

Populate the town with several recognizable gunslingers.

Create at least 6 duel opponents plus the final boss.

Give each opponent:

- a name,
- distinct clothing/colors,
- a recognizable location or role,
- different reaction speed,
- slightly different accuracy or behavior.

Examples of archetypes:

- nervous rookie,
- saloon gambler,
- outlaw,
- sheriff's deputy,
- veteran gunslinger,
- notorious bandit.

Do not simply clone exactly the same opponent with different colors.

Their difficulty should increase meaningfully.

---

# DUEL SYSTEM

The duel is the central mechanic of the game.

Treat it as a proper state machine.

A duel should progress through clear states such as:

1. challenge
2. accept/start
3. characters take position
4. face-off
5. countdown/tension
6. DRAW signal
7. shooting window
8. hit/miss resolution
9. result
10. return to exploration

Do not allow these phases to overlap incorrectly.

---

# CHALLENGING AN OPPONENT

Approaching a valid opponent should clearly indicate that they can be challenged.

For example:

`Challenge Buck?`

Starting the duel moves both characters into predefined positions facing each other.

Do not start the shooting phase immediately.

Allow a brief cinematic pause to establish the confrontation.

---

# THE DRAW RULE

This rule is critical.

Neither participant may legally shoot before the DRAW signal.

The sequence should create tension.

For example:

READY...

...

DRAW!

Do NOT make the delay before DRAW perfectly predictable every time.

Use a reasonable randomized delay so the player cannot simply memorize a fixed timing.

However, it must remain fair.

---

# EARLY SHOTS

If the PLAYER fires before DRAW:

- immediately declare a false start,
- the player loses that duel.

Clearly communicate what happened.

For example:

`TOO EARLY!`

Do not silently ignore the input.

The AI opponent must also obey the same rule.

An enemy must NEVER shoot before DRAW.

This is a core gameplay invariant.

---

# REACTION TIME

After DRAW, both gunslingers may shoot.

Opponent difficulty should primarily be influenced by believable variables such as:

- reaction delay,
- aiming delay,
- accuracy,
- probability of hesitation.

Early enemies should provide a generous window.

Later enemies should become noticeably quicker.

The final opponent should be challenging but humanly beatable.

Do not create impossible reaction times.

---

# SHOOTING

Implement actual 3D shooting logic.

The game should determine whether a shot hits or misses based on the player's aim.

Do not secretly make every shot hit.

Provide clear feedback:

- muzzle flash,
- smoke,
- recoil,
- gunshot sound if audio is available,
- impact effect,
- hit reaction.

Keep violence cartoon-like.

No blood or gore is necessary.

---

# AIMING

During a duel, the player must have meaningful control over aim.

On desktop this may use mouse input.

On touch, dragging should allow comfortable aim adjustment.

Use a visible but unobtrusive reticle.

The player should not need pixel-perfect accuracy.

Enemy hitboxes should be forgiving enough for an arcade game.

---

# DUEL OUTCOMES

Possible outcomes include:

- player shoots first and hits → win
- player fires too early → loss
- opponent shoots first and hits → loss
- player fires and misses → opponent may still fire
- opponent misses → player has another opportunity
- both actions occur very close together → resolve deterministically and sensibly

Avoid race-condition-like outcomes where the result appears arbitrary.

---

# PLAYER DEFEAT

Losing a duel should not reload the entire page.

Use a short defeat sequence.

Then return the player to a sensible location.

A defeat may cost some money or simply increase a loss/death statistic.

Do not erase all game progress.

---

# REWARDS

Winning a duel gives:

- money,
- reputation,
- progression toward tougher opponents.

Display the reward clearly.

Example:

`+ $25`
`+ 10 Reputation`

---

# PROGRESSION

Create a lightweight progression system.

The player should not be able to challenge the final gunslinger immediately.

Require the player to build reputation by defeating opponents.

For example:

- several normal gunslingers available initially,
- tougher opponents unlock after enough reputation,
- final showdown unlocks once the player becomes sufficiently notorious.

Keep the system understandable.

Do not turn it into a complicated RPG.

---

# MONEY

Money should have at least one meaningful use.

Implement a simple store or upgrade system.

Possible upgrades include:

- slightly faster aim movement,
- improved revolver accuracy,
- cosmetic hat,
- cosmetic outfit,
- different revolver appearance.

Do not create upgrades that make the duel mechanic trivial.

Reaction timing should remain the core skill.

---

# SALOON

Make the saloon a visually important landmark.

Ideally the player can enter it, but a convincing exterior interaction is acceptable if an interior would compromise the rest of the game's quality.

It can contain:

- a gunslinger,
- piano music or visual piano,
- tables,
- bar,
- swinging doors,
- NPCs.

Prioritize polish over unnecessary interior complexity.

---

# NPC LIFE

Add lightweight ambient NPC behavior so the town does not feel dead.

Examples:

- walking between locations,
- standing near buildings,
- chatting,
- sitting,
- wandering,
- reacting when a duel starts.

NPCs do not need sophisticated simulation.

They should simply make the town feel inhabited.

---

# FINAL GUNSLINGER

Create a memorable final opponent.

Give this character:

- unique appearance,
- unique name,
- special introduction,
- intimidating but fair reaction time,
- distinctive duel arena or location.

The final showdown should feel more dramatic than regular encounters.

Use camera framing, sound, lighting or environmental composition to reinforce the moment.

---

# WIN CONDITION

Defeating the final gunslinger completes the game.

Display a proper completion screen.

Include at minimum:

WILD WEST CHAMPION

and stats such as:

- total play time,
- duels won,
- duels lost,
- fastest duel,
- money earned.

Provide:

`PLAY AGAIN`

Restarting must reset progression correctly.

---

# HUD

During exploration show a clean HUD containing:

- money,
- reputation,
- current objective.

Do not cover too much of the screen.

On mobile, keep the HUD away from touch controls.

During duels, simplify the UI and emphasize:

- opponent name,
- duel state,
- reticle,
- DRAW signal.

---

# OBJECTIVES

The game should always give the player some idea of what to do next.

Examples:

`Find a gunslinger to challenge`

`Earn 50 Reputation`

`The Sheriff wants to duel`

`Final showdown unlocked`

Avoid leaving the player wandering with no indication of how progression works.

---

# AUDIO

Use audio where practical.

Important sounds include:

- revolver shot,
- dry fire if relevant,
- duel start cue,
- DRAW cue,
- hit/miss feedback,
- reward sound,
- ambient western atmosphere.

Browser autoplay restrictions must be respected.

Initialize audio from a user interaction such as START.

If external audio assets are unavailable, use generated/simple audio or ensure the game remains polished without them.

---

# STARTING EXPERIENCE

On initial load, show a polished start screen.

Something similar to:

WILD WEST SHOWDOWN

Become the fastest gunslinger in town.

Explore.
Challenge rivals.
Wait for DRAW.
Shoot first.

Desktop:
WASD + Mouse

Touch:
Joystick + Drag + Action

START

Do not overwhelm the player with instructions.

Teach additional mechanics through the game.

---

# POLISH

Once the complete game loop works, spend meaningful effort polishing it.

Look for opportunities such as:

- satisfying camera transitions,
- character poses,
- recoil animation,
- holster/draw animation,
- dust particles,
- tumbleweeds,
- bullet impact effects,
- signs moving in the wind,
- readable shadows,
- sunset/warm lighting,
- duel music sting,
- NPC reactions,
- satisfying reward feedback.

Do not prioritize decorative polish before the core game works.

---

# GAME FEEL

The player should feel:

- anticipation before DRAW,
- urgency immediately after DRAW,
- satisfaction from landing a shot,
- progression from defeating increasingly difficult gunslingers.

A technically correct but dull duel system is not enough.

Tune timing and presentation.

---

# PERFORMANCE

The game must run smoothly on:

- a modern desktop browser,
- a reasonably recent iPad.

Avoid excessive:

- draw calls,
- dynamic lights,
- high-poly geometry,
- huge textures,
- unnecessary post-processing.

Use lightweight techniques appropriate for the blocky visual style.

---

# ROBUSTNESS

Test especially:

- starting and ending duels repeatedly,
- firing too early,
- firing immediately after DRAW,
- missing shots,
- opponent misses,
- player defeat,
- opponent defeat,
- entering another duel after the previous one,
- touch controls,
- simultaneous touch inputs,
- camera state after duels,
- buying an upgrade,
- progression unlocks,
- final showdown,
- restarting after completion.

The game must remain playable after many consecutive duels.

Do not leave stale timers or event handlers from previous encounters.

A previous duel must never unexpectedly fire an event during a later duel.

---

# REQUIRED COMPLETE PLAYTHROUGH

Before declaring the project complete, perform a full logical review of this sequence:

1. Start the game.
2. Explore the town.
3. Find an opponent.
4. Challenge them.
5. Enter the duel.
6. Wait for DRAW.
7. Win a duel.
8. Receive a reward.
9. Lose a duel and recover correctly.
10. Fire too early and verify the penalty.
11. Fight multiple different opponents.
12. Earn reputation.
13. Spend money.
14. Unlock stronger opponents.
15. Unlock the final gunslinger.
16. Complete the final showdown.
17. Reach the victory screen.
18. Restart.
19. Verify that a new game starts correctly.

If any step is impossible or obviously broken, the game is not finished.

# FINAL REVIEW PHASE

Once you believe the game is complete, STOP adding new features.

Switch to review mode.

Inspect the game as if it were created by another developer.

Update `REVIEW.md` with concrete findings categorized roughly as:

- Critical
- Gameplay
- Mobile
- Visual
- Polish

Pay particular attention to whether the game technically implements a requirement but does so poorly.

Examples:

- duel works but timing feels bad,
- opponent AI shoots unfairly,
- touch aiming technically works but is uncomfortable,
- map exists but feels empty,
- upgrades exist but have no meaningful effect,
- camera frequently clips through buildings.

Then fix the important findings.

Repeat review and repair until there are no known critical problems.

Do not declare success because the application loads or because all requested systems have corresponding source files.

The game is complete only when it feels like a coherent playable product.

The intended player experience must work from beginning to end:

explore → challenge → tension → DRAW → shoot → win/lose → progress → improve → face stronger rivals → final showdown → victory.

Build the best complete game you can, and use the repository itself to preserve your plan and project state throughout the work.
